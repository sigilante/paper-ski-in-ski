\begin{abstract}
We build a symbolic step-evaluator for SKI combinatory logic as a closed
term of the basis \texttt{\{S, K, I\}}: 618 atoms, all recursion
through \texttt{Y}, no host support past the one-time encoding of the
input. Run on an encoding of itself running on a base term, it reaches
weak head normal form in 504,930 contractions and agrees with the host's
own reduction. The artifact is small enough to be read whole, and three
things about it are worth reporting. First, a structural census over the
\texttt{\{B, C, K, W\}} basis shows the representation layer is
affine (weakening only) while the interpreter core needs contraction at
exactly seven variable occurrences; representation needs weakening,
iteration needs contraction, and contraction alone already costs
normalization. Second, extending the object language with a fifth
constructor forces the interpreter's result type to grow, and whether
the new outcome is exposed as a value or absorbed into host divergence
is a single-arm edit at one named site, measured at four atoms versus
seven in this file, with the two interpreters provably agreeing on every
input that never reaches the site. We read this as a minimal executable
instance of metacircular evaluation in the sense of Nock's
\texttt{+mock}, and we state precisely where the analogy breaks: Nock's
virtualization is free because formulas are nouns, while SKI must pay
one external quoting step, which no SKI term can perform (Jay \&
Given-Wilson 2011, Thm. 3.2). Third, we build the analogy out: a
nonlocal scry site in the style of opcode 12, with the resolver passed
as an argument; a blocking version with a namespace that grows across
re-runs; and a namespace over arbitrary compound paths keyed by a
240-atom structural-equality combinator, which corrects an earlier claim
that such a namespace was out of reach.
\end{abstract}

Metacircular evaluation permits the separation of the evaluation
mechanism from the object language, allowing for controlled
experimentation with different evaluation strategies and the observation
of intermediate states without affecting the host environment.  An
interpreter can be employed to handle states such as partial evaluation
without interfering with the host environment, particularly in cases in
which the host environment itself may not be capable of representations
such as partially evaluated terms, intermediate representations of the
object language, crashes, and other exceptional states.

We construct a symbolic step-evaluator for the SKI combinator calculus
as a closed term of the basis \texttt{\{S, K, I\}}.  This evaluator performs
symbolic evaluation by repeatedly applying the reduction rules of the
SKI calculus until a normal form is reached.  Because the interpreter is
capable of symbolic representation beyond simply the basis \texttt{\{S, K, I\}},
we can add in functionality such as handling additional combinators,
supporting custom reduction strategies, and managing exceptional states
like crashes, undefined terms, or as-yet-unknown referentially
transparent lookups.

\section{Metacircular Evaluation}

While many languages nominally support it, metacircular evaluation as a
first-class strategy has only occasionally been applied outside
of the LISP family of languages, where it has been used to implement
features such as macro systems, partial evaluation, and sandboxed
execution environments.  Essentially, each nested interpreter acts as a
virtual machine for the next level of interpretation.  LISP's
homoiconic nature is particularly apt for such virtualization, since the
interpreter can directly treat its interpretand as data, enabling
LISP's powerful reflective and metaprogramming capabilities.

The lineage runs from \citet{McCarthy1960}, whose universal function
\texttt{eval} was written in the language it evaluates, and from the Lisp
1.5 manual \citep{Lisp15}, which specifies the language by that function.
\citet{Reynolds1972} gave the form its critical treatment: a definitional
interpreter written in the style of its own object language is
metacircular, and it inherits the defining language's evaluation order
unless that order is made explicit. The present work meets that
observation directly in \S\ref{fuel-and-the-third-outcome}, where the
object-level laziness of \texttt{K} is inherited rather than implemented.
\citet{SteeleSussman1978} and \citet{SICP} made the metacircular
evaluator a standard instrument for experimenting with semantics, which
is the use it is put to here.

The Nock combinator calculus explicitly adopted self-application as a
fundamental mechanism for metacircular evaluation from its earliest
inception.  This mechanism was originally intended as a ``hyper-Turing''
operator \citep{Davis2025}, in which referentially transparent queries could answer
with a value, a permanent unavailability, or a deferral.

The tower as such enters with \citet{Smith1984}, whose 3-Lisp runs each
level of a reflective tower on an interpreter at the level above;
\citet{WandFriedman1988} gave the tower a non-reflective description, and
\citet{Asai1996} the language Black, in whose style Amin and Rompf's
Purple is cast.

\citet{AminRompf2018} treat the tower itself as the
object of study. Their multi-level $\lambda$-calculus provides staging
constructs and stage polymorphism, so that a single evaluator acts as an
interpreter or as a compiler depending on runtime parameters, and their
metacircular evaluator Pink collapses arbitrarily many levels of
self-interpretation into one compiled program; correctness of
self-interpretation and multi-level Jones optimality are stated without
formal proof but backed by experiment (their Propositions~4.2 and~4.6).
Semantic modifications introduced at any level of the tower, such as
instrumentation, CPS conversion, or reflection in the style of Black,
survive the collapse. Their objective is performance, the removal of
interpretive overhead, and their semantics is value-producing throughout:
there is no fuel, no failure, and no outcome that has to be represented
rather than computed. The present work is close to complementary. We keep
the levels and pay for them
(\S\ref{virtualized-evaluation-read-against-mock}), and we ask what an
interpreter does at the site where its object language gains a construct
whose meaning the interpreter must author
(\S\ref{authored-failure-one-site}), a question that does not arise in a
setting without a third outcome. What the two share is the shape of the
mechanism: an interpreter that is an ordinary program in the calculus it
interprets, with one place at which its behaviour is substituted, which is
the shape \S\ref{substitutability-as-an-argument-the-scry-site} builds on.

In the $\lambda$-calculus proper, \citet{Barendregt1991} states the
requirement on a self-interpreter, $\mathbf{E}\,\ulcorner M\urcorner = M$,
and notes that the coding $\ulcorner\cdot\urcorner$ is a metalevel
operation rather than a term; that boundary is the one
\S\ref{encoding-is-external-decoding-is-internal} locates for SKI with
Jay and Given-Wilson's theorem. \citet{BerarducciBohm1993} show that the
choice of encoding is load-bearing, obtaining a self-interpreter that
itself has a normal form, and \citet{BrownPalsberg2016} carry
self-interpretation into System~F$_\omega$, where the barrier is typing
rather than quotation. The specialization of an interpreter to a fixed
program is Futamura's first projection \citep{Futamura1971};
\citet{JonesGomardSestoft1993} is the standard account, and
\citet{Jones1996} names the optimality condition a specializer must meet
to remove interpretive overhead entirely, a condition Amin and Rompf
claim and this work does not.

The question which motivated the current line of research was how
amenable the SKI combinator calculus is to metacircular evaluation,
and what design patterns and implementation techniques could facilitate
such evaluation.  We do not anticipate such a line of inquiry to be
competitively efficient versus other combinator systems or higher-level
representations, but rather as a tool for exploring the metacircular
evaluation process itself.

\section{Encoding is external, decoding is
internal}\label{encoding-is-external-decoding-is-internal}

\texttt{S K K} and \texttt{I} agree on every argument, yet as terms of
weak combinatory logic they are distinct normal forms, 3 atoms against
1. A \texttt{quote} function from live terms to Scott-encoded data must
tell them apart, and no SKI term can. Jay \& Given-Wilson, \emph{A
combinatory account of internal structure} (JSL 76(3), 2011), Theorem
3.2, \S{}3: factorisation of SK-combinators is a symbolic computation that
is not representable. The theorem is stated for \texttt{\{S, K\}}; its
proof goes through unchanged with \texttt{I} primitive. Quotation
follows from it in two lines. If \texttt{Q M $\twoheadrightarrow$ $\langle$M$\rangle$} were definable,
then under the translation to $\lambda$-calculus \texttt{$\llbracket$Q$\rrbracket$ ($\lambda$x.x)} is one
fixed term, yet \texttt{Q (S K X)} yields \texttt{$\langle$S K X$\rangle$}, from
which \texttt{X} is extractable by Scott dispatch, so every \texttt{X}
would share a reduct, contradicting that \texttt{S} and \texttt{K} are
distinct normal forms. (Equivalently, \texttt{Q} together with this
artifact's own \texttt{UQ} would build a factorisation combinator,
contradicting Theorem 3.2 directly.)

This is why \texttt{enc()} and \texttt{encQ()} are Python. Everything on
the other side of that boundary, \texttt{UQ}, \texttt{whnfF}, their
self-application, and everything in \S\ref{substitutability-as-an-argument-the-scry-site} including path equality, is
ordinary \texttt{\{S, K, I\}}. Quoting is a one-time cost paid to
enter the representation, and every level above it is closed.
\texttt{ski-in-ski-aviary.ipynb} makes this its explicit stopping point
(``the wall'', its \S{}8).

\section{Where the structural cost
lives}\label{where-the-structural-cost-lives}

\texttt{census.py} and \texttt{bckw\_census.py} rebuild each component
over \texttt{\{B, C, K, W\}} and record which structural rule each
needs, weakening (\texttt{K}) or contraction (\texttt{W}). The
measurement that is independent of the abstraction algorithm is the
\emph{occurrence} census (\texttt{census.py} \S{}1): a component needs
weakening iff some binder occurs zero times and contraction iff some
binder occurs more than once. The atom census is convention-relative and
the scripts say so: with \texttt{I} primitive the interpreter core shows
7 \texttt{W} atoms, with \texttt{I = W K} it shows 10, and under the
latter convention even the encoding layer shows 4.

\textbf{The representation layer is affine.} All ten Scott constructors
(\texttt{enc\textless{}S\textgreater{}},
\texttt{enc\textless{}K\textgreater{}},
\texttt{enc\textless{}I\textgreater{}},
\texttt{enc\textless{}app\textgreater{}}, \texttt{nil}, \texttt{cons},
\texttt{nothing}, \texttt{just}, \texttt{zero}, \texttt{suc}) have no
binder occurring twice: a constructor selects one continuation and
discards the rest, which is weakening by construction. The
representation lives in BCK.

\textbf{The interpreter core needs contraction at five of eight
components, seven surplus occurrences in total:} \texttt{UQ}'s
\texttt{f} (twice, in \texttt{(f t)(f u)}), the spine generator's
\texttt{acc} (four times), the rebuild generator's \texttt{h} (twice),
the \texttt{S}-step arm's \texttt{z} (twice, in \texttt{x z (y z)},
which is the \texttt{S} rule itself reappearing one level up), and the
fuel loop's \texttt{m} (twice). Surplus occurrences sum to
\texttt{1 + 3 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 7}, and any BCKW abstraction must
perform at least that many contractions. The differential check (18/18
SKI and BCKW abstractions extensionally agree; the purity guard accepts
pure-basis terms and rejects mixed ones) confirms the abstractions are
correct; it does not bear on the census, and is not cited for it.

\textbf{Why each rule is needed.} Removing weakening removes dispatch: a
Scott constructor \emph{is} an n-ary discard. Removing contraction
removes iteration: \texttt{Y} duplicates its argument, and the affine
fragment BCK is strongly normalizing, so it has no fixpoint combinator
and no fuel loop. (The fuel itself is a Scott numeral, which is affine;
iteration here comes from \texttt{Y}, not from the numeral.)
Self-interpretation therefore needs both rules for two different
reasons. The asymmetry is worth stating exactly: contraction alone
already costs normalization. \texttt{(S I I)(S I I)} contains no
\texttt{K}, and in BCKW \texttt{W W W} reduces to itself with no
weakening anywhere; the relevant fragment BCIW is not normalizing while
BCK is. So the rule that buys iteration is the rule that costs
normalization, and weakening is bought for representation, not for
divergence.

\section{Fuel and the third outcome}\label{fuel-and-the-third-outcome}

An unfueled interpreter's outcome space is
\texttt{\{value, divergence\}}, and divergence cannot be observed from
inside a finite run. Fuel converts it to \texttt{\{value, timeout\}},
both observable. The precise limitation is local: at any fixed fuel
\texttt{k}, one observation does not separate a divergent term from one
that needs more than \texttt{k} steps. \texttt{$\Omega$} reports
\texttt{TIMEOUT} at every \texttt{k}; \texttt{I$^{31}$K} reports
\texttt{TIMEOUT} for \texttt{k $\leq$ 31} and a value from
\texttt{k = 32} on (31 contractions, and fuel \texttt{k} permits
\texttt{k} step-attempts of which the last must be the no-redex check).
Convergence is semi-decidable by raising \texttt{k}; divergence is not.
\texttt{K I $\Omega$} still discards \texttt{$\Omega$} unevaluated under the
emulator, exactly as under host reduction, because the object-level
\texttt{K} rule fires before the interpreter recurses into the argument:
object-level laziness is inherited, not re-implemented.

Amin \& Rompf's framework (\emph{Collapsing Towers of Interpreters},
POPL 2018) supports a rich family of semantic modifications across tower
levels: instrumentation and CPS transformation (their \S{}4.2),
\texttt{EM}, \texttt{call/\allowbreak{}cc} and \texttt{shift}/\texttt{reset} at user
level, reflection à la Black, and meta-level undo (\S{}5 to \S{}7). Their
correctness and optimality properties (Props. 4.2 to 4.7, of which 4.5
is a definition) are, in their words, ``stated without formal proofs but
backed by experimental evidence'' (\S{}4.1, p. 11). The word ``fuel'' does
not occur in the paper, and no outcome-type that must grow when the
object language gains a construct is discussed. Their collapse is a
collapse of value-producing computation; the third outcome is outside
the space their equivalences range over.

\section{Authored failure: one site}\label{authored-failure-one-site}

Extend the object language with a fifth constructor \texttt{Err}, with
no object-level contraction rule. Its Scott encoding is unremarkable,
five continuations instead of four. What is interesting is what the
interpreter does when the spine walker reaches \texttt{Err} in head
position, and that is a choice made at one site:

\begin{verbatim}
D("errArmAbs", ["acc"], a("errd"))      # observable: crash becomes a value
D("errArmOmg", ["acc"], OMEGA)           # hidden: crash becomes host divergence
\end{verbatim}

(\texttt{tower\_harness.py} lines 217 to 218, verbatim.) Expanded, the
arms are \texttt{K (K (K I))} at 4 tree-atoms and
\texttt{K (S I I (S I I))} at 7; the two full emulators
\texttt{wf5Abs} and \texttt{wf5Omg} measure 768 and 771 and differ at
exactly this one definition site. The measurement is of this file, not a
minimum: the \texttt{K} wrapper on \texttt{$\Omega$} is unnecessary, since
\texttt{$\Omega$ acc} diverges as \texttt{$\Omega$} does, and a 6-atom arm reproduces
the table below cell for cell (\texttt{review/\allowbreak{}omg6.py}). Even the 4
depends on the abstraction algorithm's $\eta$ and \texttt{I} shortcuts. The
honest statement is a single-digit-atom edit at one named site.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{build/fig/fig-arms.pdf}
\caption{The two arms of the authored site.
\protect\texttt{errArmAbs = K (K (K I))}, 4 atoms, exposes the
crash as a value;
\protect\texttt{errArmOmg = K (S I I (S I I))}, 7 atoms, absorbs
it into divergence. The two emulators differ by exactly these atoms.}
\end{figure}

\texttt{$\Omega$ = (S I I)(S I I)} is one of the smallest divergent terms
in this basis, not the unique one. Exhaustive enumeration of every
\texttt{\{S, K, I\}} application tree of at most 6 atoms
(\texttt{review/\allowbreak{}brute2.py}; the counts match
\texttt{3$^{n}$ $\cdot$ Catalan(n$-$1)}, so the enumeration is complete) finds that
all 3,873 terms of at most 5 atoms reach a normal form, and exactly five
terms of 6 atoms do not, under both ``no normal form'' and ``no weak
head normal form'': \texttt{S I I (S I I)},
\texttt{S (S I)(S I) I}, \texttt{S S S (S I) I},
\texttt{S S I (S I) I}, and \texttt{S (S S) S I I}. (Tromp
notes that in \texttt{\{S, K\}} alone the smallest unsolvable term is
\texttt{S S K (S (S S K))}, 7 atoms; consistent with the
enumeration, since all five 6-atom divergent terms contain \texttt{I}.)

Re-run and verified:

\begin{longtable}[]{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.32\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
object term & exposing (\texttt{errArmAbs}) & hiding
(\texttt{errArmOmg}) \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
\texttt{Err} & ERR & host diverges \\
\texttt{Err K} & ERR & host diverges \\
\texttt{K I Err} & VAL & VAL \\
\texttt{I K} & VAL & VAL \\
\texttt{$\Omega$} & TIME & TIME \\
\end{longtable}

``Host diverges'' is the host reducer's 400,000-step cap being hit; it
is not a detected non-termination, though it is certain here because the
arm \emph{is} \texttt{$\Omega$}.

\textbf{Lemma (faithfulness off the site).} For every object term that
never brings \texttt{Err} into head position during its reduction,
\texttt{wf5Abs} and \texttt{wf5Omg} produce the same result. The two
definitions differ only in \texttt{errArm}, which is referenced only
from the \texttt{Err} arm of the step function, which the spine walker
selects only when the head leaf is \texttt{Err}. On any other input the
two are the same program. The table's \texttt{K I Err} row is the
instance where \texttt{Err} is present but discarded by object-level
laziness before it is ever in head position.

Adding a constructor does not add a meaning. The meaning is authored
downstream by whoever writes the interpreter, and here the whole
decision is legible as a one-arm diff with a proof that nothing else
changed.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Virtualized evaluation, read against
\texttt{+mock}}{Virtualized evaluation, read against +mock}}\label{virtualized-evaluation-read-against-mock}

Nock's \texttt{+mock} is not interesting because Nock can interpret
Nock; that follows once a formula computes \texttt{nock}, since a
formula already is a noun. Its interest is that a formula can be run
through a \emph{substituted} interpreter, one that resolves a scry
differently or catches a crash and returns it as a \texttt{unit}, while
agreement with the reference interpreter holds wherever the substitution
is not exercised, and the substitution is confined to a named site.

Read that way, \S\ref{authored-failure-one-site} is the SKI analogue of \texttt{+mock}, not \S\ref{the-artifact}'s tower.
The tower demonstrates self-interpretation and fidelity; a general
version of that demonstration exists for value-producing towers (Amin \&
Rompf, Props. 4.2 and 4.6). What \texttt{errArmAbs} versus
\texttt{errArmOmg} demonstrates is the thing \texttt{+mock} is for: the
same program, the same interpreter apart from one arm, different
observable outcomes because that arm decides what one outcome category
is allowed to be. The lemma in \S\ref{authored-failure-one-site} is the faithfulness requirement,
virtualized-with-no-substitution equals real, and \texttt{pipeline.py}'s
Stage 6 check and T0 to T2 are its instances.

\textbf{Where the analogy breaks.} \texttt{+mock} never needs a quoting
step, because a Nock formula already is the noun handed to the alternate
interpreter. SKI is not homoiconic: \texttt{S}, \texttt{K}, \texttt{I}
are opaque, and \S\ref{encoding-is-external-decoding-is-internal} is precisely the statement that no SKI term can turn
an arbitrary SKI term into data about itself. So SKI-in-SKI is not
symmetric the way Nock-on-Nock is. It is one unavoidable non-SKI quoting
step, and only above that boundary is virtualization SKI-native and
closed under further quoting. Identifying which half of ``virtualized
evaluation'' transfers to a non-homoiconic calculus is worth more than
the analogy alone.

\textbf{Depth is bounded for the ordinary reason.} Layer one costs 340
contractions to emulate a 1-step reduction; layer two costs 504,930,
about 1,485 times layer one. That is the familiar roughly-10$^{3}$$\times$ per-layer
overhead of naive self-interpretation, not a measurement this artifact
is claiming (\texttt{GATE1-RELATED-WORK.md}, A3). A third level was not
attempted. Real Nock-on-Nock virtualization has the same character,
which is why jets exist.

\section{Substitutability as an argument: the scry
site}\label{substitutability-as-an-argument-the-scry-site}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Synchronous:
\texttt{scry\_harness.py}}{Synchronous: scry\_harness.py}}\label{synchronous-scry_harnesspy}

\S\ref{authored-failure-one-site}'s substitution is a compile-time choice between two source variants.
Real scry substitution takes the resolver as an argument.
\texttt{scry\_harness.py} builds that: a separate 5-symbol encoding
\texttt{\{S, K, I, App, Scry\}} (parallel to
\texttt{\{S, K, I, App, Err\}}, not stacked on it; \texttt{Scry} has
arity 1 like \texttt{I}), and a fueled loop
\texttt{wfQ E n \textless{}t\textgreater{}} whose extra argument
\texttt{E} is an ordinary Scott-encoded \texttt{\{S, K, I\}} partial
function from paths to \texttt{Maybe} values. When the spine walker
reaches \texttt{Scry p} it applies \texttt{E} to \texttt{p}. A hit
splices the answer in and reduction continues. A miss is kept as a
third, distinguishable step outcome, reusing T3's
\texttt{stepped}/\texttt{done}/\texttt{errd}, rather than folded into
the pre-existing \texttt{Maybe}, which would conflate ``the oracle had
no answer'' with ``genuinely stuck''. At the outer result type the three
points are \texttt{\{value-or-stuck, miss, timeout\}}: \texttt{done}
maps to \texttt{rVal}, so stuck and value share a tag there, and the
stuck/miss distinction is a step-level fact.

and unreduced. Verified against three oracles (answer every path by

only \texttt{K}):

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Blocking:
\texttt{scry\_namespace.py}}{Blocking: scry\_namespace.py}}\label{blocking-scry_namespacepy}

A resolver that always answers is still not \texttt{+mock}'s scry: a
real lookup can fail to answer \emph{yet}. \texttt{scry\_namespace.py}
builds the cheapest faithful version, option (a) of
\texttt{SCRY-BLOCKING-DESIGN.md}, written before any of it was
implemented. The resolver's type grows from 2-way \texttt{Maybe} to
3-way (\texttt{oJust}/\texttt{oNothing}/\texttt{oNotYet}) and the
interpreter's outcome types grow to match (\texttt{pendingN} at the step
level, \texttt{rBlockN} at the result level). This is the phenomenon of
\S\ref{authored-failure-one-site} again, deliberately re-triggered by our own extension rather than
discovered.

Resuming a block captures no continuation. Because reduction is pure and
deterministic, the driver re-runs the whole term from the top under an
oracle that now also answers the missing fact. Two things make that
sound, and both need saying. First, the re-run's steps \emph{strictly
before} the block are identical to the first run's only if the new
oracle agrees with the old one on every path the old one answered; that
holds because the namespace is append-only, and an oracle that revised
an answer would break it. Second, re-running is not fuel-neutral. Each
round replays the previous one and then takes at least one more step, so
with a fixed fuel budget a block can turn into a timeout on the next
round:

\begin{verbatim}
Scry (S K), fact (S K)=I, fuel=1 -> [(1, 'BLOCK on S K'), (2, 'TIME')]
Scry (S K), fact (S K)=I, fuel=2 -> [(1, 'BLOCK on S K'), (2, 'VAL')]
\end{verbatim}

Recomputation is charged for. The two heavier resume strategies in the
design note, resume from the top-level term and full CPS (the move Amin
\& Rompf's CPS evaluator makes, their \S{}4.2.2), remain unbuilt.

What is SKI and what is host, stated plainly: \texttt{wfN} and each
round's oracle term are closed \texttt{\{S, K, I\}}; the fact table
standing in for an external source of truth, the resume loop, and the
``stuck'' detection are Python.

The demonstration that matters is that the namespace accumulates. With
an empty namespace and a fact table
\texttt{\{S $\mapsto$ Scry K, K $\mapsto$ I\}}, \texttt{Scry S} blocks on
\texttt{S} (round 1), learns it, re-runs, and the newly substituted
\texttt{Scry K} blocks on \texttt{K} (round 2), a fact forced by the
result of resolving the first, before round 3 reaches the value
\texttt{I}. A path with no entry blocks into a distinguishable stuck
state; fuel exhaustion stays distinguishable from a block at every
round.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Compound paths:
\texttt{scry\_paths.py}}{Compound paths: scry\_paths.py}}\label{compound-paths-scry_pathspy}

\texttt{scry\_namespace.py} dispatched on a path's head leaf only, so
its namespace could not tell \texttt{S K} from \texttt{S K K}. An
earlier draft of this paper, and that file's docstring, said this was
forced: that comparing arbitrary encoded paths was ``the
structural-equality problem Jay \& Given-Wilson show plain SK cannot
solve.'' That was wrong, and the review caught it. Theorem 3.2 is about
factorising \emph{live} combinators. The path the oracle receives is
Scott-encoded \emph{data}, a tree with five leaf tags, and equality on
such data is an ordinary structural recursion. Jay \& Given-Wilson say
so themselves (preprint pp. 7 to 8): once a term is encoded,
factorisation ``is a routine list operation.'' What SK cannot do is
produce the encoding from a live term, which is \S\ref{encoding-is-external-decoding-is-internal}, and it is the only
thing SK cannot do here.

\texttt{scry\_paths.py} builds \texttt{EQ5}, structural equality on the
five-constructor encoding, at 240 tree-atoms in \texttt{\{S, K, I\}},
and an oracle that compares the incoming path against each known path in
turn:

\begin{verbatim}
p |-> EQ5 p <k1> (oJust <a1>) (EQ5 p <k2> (oJust <a2>) (... oNotYet))
\end{verbatim}

to conversion would be the word problem for SKI, undecidable;
normalizing the path under fuel before lookup would be a fueled
approximation to it and would reintroduce the third outcome inside path
resolution. Nothing built here does either. A three-fact compound-path
oracle term measures 1,022 tree-atoms; the driver's decoder from an
encoded payload back to an object term, needed to consult the Python
fact table, is a behavioral fresh-marker probe, and its round trip is
checked.

\section{Prior art, and what is
claimed}\label{prior-art-and-what-is-claimed}

Self-interpretation for a Turing-complete system is old. Kleene 1936
gives the Normal Form Theorem, from which a universal function follows;
the explicit universal machine is Turing 1936; ``partial recursive'' is
Kleene 1938. Mogensen 1992 builds a compact self-interpreter and
self-reducer in untyped $\lambda$-calculus over his own term representation. Jay
\& Palsberg 2011 build a typed self-interpreter in a factorisation
calculus in the style of Jay \& Given-Wilson, a combinatory calculus
though not SK, and their distinction between self-\emph{recognisers}
(recover the term from its representation) and self-\emph{enactors}
(execute it) is the one this paper needs below.

The closest prior construction for SK is Tromp, \emph{Binary Lambda
Calculus and Combinatory Logic} (Dagstuhl Seminar Proceedings 06051,
2006; the 2023 revision at \texttt{tromp.github.io} has different sizes
and should not be cited for these). Theorem 3 gives a $\lambda$-term \texttt{F}
of size 124 with \texttt{F C ($\tilde{M}$ : N) = C M N} for the bitstring
encoding \texttt{$\tilde{M}$} of a combinator \texttt{M}; its verification is
``left as an exercise to the reader.'' Theorem 4 states, in full,
``There is a self-interpreter F for Combinatory Logic of size 263,''
obtained from Theorem 3 by improved bracket abstraction and a different
fixpoint combinator (\texttt{Y\textquotesingle{}\textquotesingle{}}); it
has no proof text beyond size bookkeeping, and the inductive proof in
the paper is for the $\lambda$-calculus interpreter of Theorem 2. With
\texttt{C = I}, \texttt{F I ($\tilde{M}$ : N) = M N} is a self-enactor,
and Tromp's \S{}4.1 uses it that way for universality. Architecturally
\texttt{F} is a decoder: it reconstructs live syntax from bits and hands
it to a continuation, and reduction then happens under the ambient
calculus's own rules. It carries no object-level \texttt{S} or
\texttt{K} rule in its body, which means it cannot implement a step
relation that differs from the host's, and that is exactly what \S\ref{authored-failure-one-site}'s
hiding arm does. The paper also contains (pp. 16 to 18)
\texttt{nfreduce}, ``a complete symbolic lambda calculus normal form
reducer'' over Scott-encoded de Bruijn terms with explicit substitution,
used as a 2,288-bit size constant: a symbolic evaluator over encoded
data, for $\lambda$-calculus, unfueled, not self-applied, and not run.

So the claim is narrow. A symbolic step-evaluator for SKI written in
$\lambda$-calculus is a textbook exercise, and bracket abstraction carries it
into \texttt{\{S, K, I\}} mechanically. What this artifact adds is the
concrete term: 618 atoms, fueled, with its step rules as data
manipulation rather than re-spliced host syntax, applied to an encoding
of itself, and executed to completion with the count reported. On top of
that term, \S\ref{where-the-structural-cost-lives}'s census, \S\ref{authored-failure-one-site}'s one-site authorship with its faithfulness
lemma, and \S\ref{substitutability-as-an-argument-the-scry-site}'s substitutable, blocking, compound-path scry site are, to
the extent of a full read of the closest papers, not found elsewhere.
Amin \& Rompf's outcome space has no failure case to author (\S\ref{fuel-and-the-third-outcome}); Jay \&
Given-Wilson's result is about what SK cannot do to live terms, not
about what an interpreter that can be written chooses to expose.

\section{Adjacent, distinct work}\label{adjacent-distinct-work}

Two lines of work in this same environment operate over a different
basis and question, and are context rather than sources:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Infinitary normal forms in the pure S-calculus} (companion
  draft, \texttt{Documents/\allowbreak{}Davis2026b}) studies \texttt{CL(S)}, the
  calculus generated by \texttt{S x y z $\to$ x z (y z)} alone, no
  \texttt{K}, no \texttt{I}, and writes out in detail (crediting the
  observation to an aside in Waldmann's thesis, \S{}7.6) that it is
  infinitarily strongly normalizing: every term has a unique, possibly
  infinite normal form, a Böhm-tree analogue with no \texttt{$\bot$} needed.
  Its slogan, that \texttt{K} ``purchases computational universality at
  the cost of infinitary semantic coherence,'' is the same style of
  result as \S\ref{where-the-structural-cost-lives} here, attributing a capability and its cost to one
  specific structural rule. Note that the \texttt{$\Omega$} of \S\ref{authored-failure-one-site} contains
  \texttt{I} and is not a \texttt{CL(S)} term; that paper's divergent
  example is the pure-\texttt{S} term \texttt{S S S (S S) S S},
  and it is explicit that once \texttt{K} is present the Böhm-tree
  theory needs quotienting and \texttt{$\bot$}. The two papers treat
  divergence with different tools, denotational there and operational
  (fuel) here, on different fragments.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{ssearch}} (\texttt{Documents/\allowbreak{}ssearch}) is a
  computational classification of divergent pure-\texttt{S} orbits by
  the structure of their infinite spine dynamics, in service of the
  Wolfram \texttt{S}-combinator universality question. Same author,
  adjacent, not a dependency.
\end{itemize}
